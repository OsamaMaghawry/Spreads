How the expected move works

The expected move turns option prices into a price range.

  1. Option prices carry the market's view of movement. The more movement traders price in, the more options cost. That priced-in movement, stated per year, is implied volatility.
  2. Scale it to the days left. Volatility grows with the square root of time, so multiplying by the square root of days over 365 turns the yearly figure into a move by the expiration you care about.
  3. Read it as a range. Today's price plus or minus that move is the one standard deviation range the market is pricing. Traders compare their strikes with it to see whether a strike sits inside or outside the range.

How to calculate the expected move

The expected move is the one standard deviation range implied by option prices. It comes from three numbers: the stock price, the implied volatility of the expiration you care about, and the days left until it.

Expected move = stock price × implied volatility × √(days to expiration / 365)
Range         = stock price ± expected move
Two sd range  = stock price ± 2 × expected move

Enter implied volatility as a percent and the calculator converts it: 30% is 0.30 in the formula. Days are calendar days, the same count as days to expiration.

Worked example

Hypothetical: a stock at $190 with 30% implied volatility on the expiration 30 days out.

$190 × 0.30 × √(30 / 365) = $190 × 0.30 × 0.2867 = $16.34

The one standard deviation range is $173.66 to $206.34. Under the model's assumptions the stock finishes inside it about 68% of the time, and inside $157.32 to $222.68 about 95% of the time.

From the straddle price

Many traders read the expected move straight off the at-the-money straddle, the call plus the put at the strike nearest the stock price. That is a quick estimate of the average size of the move. It runs smaller than one standard deviation: under the standard model a straddle costs about 0.8 of a one standard deviation move.

One sd move ≈ straddle price / 0.798   (about straddle × 1.25)

This calculator makes that conversion, so both methods answer the same question and agree with each other. Some charting sites quote 85% of the straddle instead; that is a convention, and it gives a narrower range than the one standard deviation figure.

What the expected move does not tell you

  • It is not a forecast. It is what option prices imply today, under a model that assumes moves follow a bell curve. Real moves have fatter tails: big gaps happen more often than the curve suggests.
  • It changes. Implied volatility moves every day, and drops sharply after earnings, so the range for the same expiration changes with it.
  • It is not a probability of profit. Whether a trade makes money depends on its strikes and premium. The expected move tells you where the market prices the stock, not what any position will do.

Frequently asked questions

What is the expected move in options?

The range the options market prices a stock to move within by a given expiration, usually one standard deviation either way of today's price. It comes from implied volatility, or from the price of the at-the-money straddle.

How does the expected move work?

Option prices imply how much the market expects a stock to move. Taking the implied volatility, scaling it from a year down to the days until expiration and applying it to today's price gives a dollar range either side of the price: the expected move.

What is implied volatility?

The volatility that today's option prices imply: the yearly standard deviation of the stock's moves that an option pricing model needs to arrive at the market price. Higher implied volatility means more expensive options and a wider expected move.

How do you calculate the expected move of a stock?

Multiply the stock price by the implied volatility and by the square root of the days to expiration divided by 365. A $190 stock with 30% implied volatility and 30 days left has an expected move of about $16.34 either way.

How do you calculate the expected move from a straddle?

Add the at-the-money call and put prices. That straddle price is a rough expected move on its own; dividing it by 0.798 converts it to a one standard deviation move, the same measure the implied volatility method gives.

How often does a stock stay within its expected move?

Under the model's bell curve, about 68% of the time for one standard deviation and 95% for two. Those are the model's numbers, not a promise; earnings and news produce moves outside the range more often than the curve allows.

Should I use calendar days or trading days?

This calculator uses calendar days over 365, which matches days to expiration. Some traders use trading days over 252. For the same expiration the two give close results; mixing them, such as trading days over 365, does not.

How do I find the expected move for earnings?

Use the implied volatility, or the straddle, of the first expiration after the earnings date. Most of that expiration's move is priced into the report itself, so the range can be much wider than usual.

Is the expected move the same as the probability of profit?

No. The expected move describes where the market prices the stock. A trade's chance of profit depends on its own strikes, credit or debit, and breakeven.
