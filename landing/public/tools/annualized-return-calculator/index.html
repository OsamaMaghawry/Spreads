How annualized return works

Annualizing takes three steps.

  1. Return for the period. Divide the premium by the capital the trade ties up.
  2. Scale it to a year. Multiply by 365 and divide by the days the money is tied up: how many trades of that length fit into a year.
  3. Compare. Annualized figures for trades of different lengths can be set side by side. Their period returns cannot, because a 7 day trade and a 45 day trade tie the money up for different lengths of time.

How to annualize an options return

An option seller's return depends on how long the money is tied up. Annualizing puts a 7 day trade and a 45 day trade on the same scale, so you can compare what each pays for the time it takes.

Return for the period = premium / capital
Simple annualized     = premium / capital × 365 / days
Compounded            = (1 + premium / capital) ^ (365 / days) - 1

Simple annualizing is the common convention and the one most brokers and calculators show. Compounding assumes every period's premium is added to the capital and earns at the same rate, which makes it always a little higher.

What counts as capital

Cash-secured put
The strike × 100 per contract, the cash the broker holds.
Covered call
The value of the 100 shares per contract, at the price you paid or today's price.
Credit spread
The collateral, width × 100. If your broker nets the credit, use the max loss instead. Return on risk and return on capital are different numbers for the same spread.

Worked example

Hypothetical: you collect $150.00 on a position that ties up $5,000.00 for 30 days. That is 3.0% for the period. Simple annualized: 3.0% × 365 / 30 = 36.5%. Compounded: (1.03)^(365 / 30) - 1 = 43.3%.

What annualizing does not tell you

  • It assumes repetition. The yearly figure assumes the same trade on the same terms every period, with no losses. One assignment or max loss can take back many periods of premium.
  • It is not a forecast. It describes the trade's terms, not what an account will do over a year.
  • Fees and idle cash. Commissions and the days between trades, when the cash earns nothing, are not included.

Frequently asked questions

What is annualized return on an options trade?

The return a trade earns over the time it is open, scaled to a full year so trades of different lengths can be compared. A return of 2% in 30 days is about 24.3% annualized, simple. It describes the trade's rate, not what an account will earn in a year.

How does annualizing a return work?

You take the return for the time the trade is open and scale it to 365 days. A return of 2% over 30 days becomes 2% times 365 / 30, about 24.3% annualized. Compounding instead assumes each period's premium is reinvested, which gives a slightly higher figure.

How do you annualize an option premium return?

Divide the premium by the capital it ties up, then multiply by 365 and divide by the days. $150 on $5,000 for 30 days is 3.0% for the period and 36.5% annualized.

Should I use simple or compounded annualized return?

Simple is the common convention for comparing trades and the more conservative of the two. Compounded assumes each period's premium is reinvested at the same rate, which real trading rarely allows.

What capital should I use for a credit spread?

The collateral your broker holds, usually the width times 100. If your broker nets the credit, the amount held equals the max loss, and dividing by that gives the return on risk instead.

Why do short-dated options annualize so high?

Because the same premium arrives over fewer days. A 2% period return is about 104% annualized over 7 days and 12% over 60 days. The shorter trade is not safer for it; it simply repeats more often on paper.

Should I use 365 or 252 days?

Use 365 when you count calendar days to expiration, which is how expirations are quoted. Use 252 only if you count trading days. Mixing the two overstates or understates the result.
