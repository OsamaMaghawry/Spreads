How rolling an option works

A roll is two trades sent as one order.

  1. Close the old option. Buy back the option you are short. Its gain or loss is realized on this trade.
  2. Open the new one. Sell a similar option, usually with a later expiration and often at a different strike.
  3. One net price. Both legs go in as one order. If the new option brings in more than the old one costs to close, the roll is a net credit; if less, a net debit.
  4. What you hold after. The new strike and expiration, carrying all the premium kept so far. That total sets the new breakeven.

How to calculate a roll

A roll closes the option you are short and opens another, usually further out in time and often at a different strike. It is two trades, sent as one order. These are the numbers that decide what it did:

Net credit (or debit) = new premium - cost to close
Realized on the close = original premium - cost to close
Total premium kept    = original premium - cost to close + new premium
New breakeven, put    = new strike - total premium kept
New breakeven, call   = new strike + total premium kept

The breakeven here counts everything the position has collected or paid since it was first opened, which is how most wheel traders track it. Your broker's statement records the closed option as a finished trade with its own gain or loss.

Worked example: rolling a put down and out

Hypothetical: you sold a $50 put for $1.50. The stock fell and the put now costs $2.50 to buy back. You close it and sell next month's $48 put for $3.00.

  • The roll: $3.00 - $2.50 = $0.50 credit, $50.00 per contract.
  • Realized on the old put: $1.50 - $2.50 = -$1.00, a $100.00 loss booked when it closes.
  • Total premium kept: $1.50 - $2.50 + $3.00 = $2.00, $200.00 per contract if the new put expires worthless.
  • Breakeven: was $50 - $1.50 = $48.50; now $48 - $2.00 = $46.00, $2.50 lower.

Credit and debit rolls

A roll for a net credit adds premium, which moves the breakeven in your favor even at the same strike. A roll for a net debit pays to change the strike or the date, and takes premium away. Either can still finish with a loss: rolling changes the terms, it does not undo the move that made you roll.

What the calculator does not tell you

  • The extra time at risk. A later expiration keeps the position, and its risk, open for longer.
  • Assignment. A short option in the money can be assigned at any time, before or after a roll.
  • Fees and taxes. A roll is two trades for commissions, and the closed option is a realized gain or loss.

Frequently asked questions

What does rolling an option mean?

Closing an option position and opening a similar one, usually with a later expiration and often at a different strike, in one order. Option sellers roll to give a trade more time, or to move a short strike further from the stock price.

How does rolling an option work?

You buy back the option you are short and sell a new one, usually further out in time, in a single order. The difference between the two prices is the roll's net credit or debit, and the new position's breakeven counts all the premium kept across both.

How do you calculate the net credit of a roll?

Subtract the cost of buying back your current option from the premium of the new one. Selling the new put for $3.00 after buying the old one back for $2.50 is a $0.50 net credit, $50 per contract.

What does rolling do to my breakeven?

For a short put the new breakeven is the new strike minus all the premium kept across both options; for a short call it is the new strike plus it. A credit roll to a lower put strike moves the breakeven down on both counts.

What is the difference between rolling for a credit and a debit?

A credit roll takes in more for the new option than the old one costs to close, adding to the premium kept. A debit roll costs money, usually to move the strike further away, and reduces it.

Is a roll one trade or two?

Two: a closing trade on the old option and an opening trade on the new one. Brokers let you send both as one order with one net price, but your statement shows two trades.

Can I use this for a credit spread roll?

For the breakeven, yes: enter the net credit of each spread as its premium and the short strikes as the strikes. For the new spread's max loss, enter its strikes and the total premium kept into the credit spread calculator.

Does rolling reduce the risk of assignment?

Moving a short option further out of the money or adding time value can make early assignment less likely, but a short option in the money can be assigned on any day before it expires.
