How to calculate a cash-secured put

Selling a cash-secured put means agreeing to buy 100 shares per contract at the strike, and holding the cash to do it. You are paid the premium up front. If the stock finishes above the strike you keep the premium and the cash is released; below it, you are assigned and buy the shares.

Cash held           = strike × 100 × contracts
Return on cash      = premium / strike
Annualized          = premium / strike × 365 / days to expiration
Cost basis if assigned = strike - premium
Breakeven           = strike - premium
Max loss            = (strike - premium) × 100 × contracts

Some brokers hold the strike minus the premium you already received rather than the full strike. Measured on that smaller amount the return is premium / (strike - premium), slightly higher: 2.04% instead of 2.0% in the example below.

Worked example

Hypothetical: a stock trades at $53. You sell the $50 put expiring in 30 days for $1.00.

  • Cash held: $50 × 100 = $5,000.00. Premium: $100.00.
  • Return on cash: $1.00 / $50 = 2.0% in 30 days, 24.3% annualized.
  • If assigned: you buy at $50 but have already been paid $1.00, so your cost basis is $49.00, 7.5% below today's $53.
  • Max loss: $4,900.00, if the shares became worthless. Below $49.00 the position loses a dollar per share for every dollar the stock falls.

The wheel

A cash-secured put is the first step of the wheel: sell puts until you are assigned, then sell covered calls on the shares until they are called away, then start again. Your cost basis after assignment, strike minus premium, is the number the rest of the wheel is measured from. The covered call calculator takes it from there.

What the calculator does not tell you

  • The chance of assignment. A put closer to the stock price pays more because it is more likely to be assigned.
  • The downside after assignment. Once you own the shares their loss is yours; the premium only lowers your cost.
  • Fees, taxes and early assignment. None are included. A put deep in the money can be assigned before expiration.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate the return on a cash-secured put?

Divide the premium by the strike. A $1.00 premium on a $50 strike is 2.0% of the $5,000 held per contract. To annualize it, multiply by 365 and divide by the days to expiration.

What is my cost basis if a cash-secured put is assigned?

The strike minus the premium you received. Assigned on a $50 put you sold for $1.00, you have paid $49 a share in effect. In the US, IRS Publication 550 also treats the premium as reducing the basis of the shares when a written put is exercised.

How much cash do I need to sell a cash-secured put?

The strike times 100 for each contract. A $50 put holds $5,000. Some brokers reduce that by the premium received.

What is the breakeven on a cash-secured put?

The strike minus the premium. Above it the trade makes money at expiration; below it you own shares worth less than your cost basis.

Is selling a put the same as buying the stock lower?

Close, with two differences. If the stock never falls to the strike you keep the premium and own nothing. If it rises a lot, your gain stops at the premium, where a stock owner's would not.

Why annualize the return?

To compare puts with different expirations on equal terms. A smaller premium on a 7 day put can be a higher yearly rate than a larger one on a 45 day put. It assumes the same trade could be repeated, which no market promises.
