How to tell if a short call will be assigned early

Most options are not exercised before expiration, because exercising throws away whatever time value the option has left. The exception is a call in the money just before an ex-dividend date. A holder who exercises the day before becomes a shareholder in time to receive the dividend. If the dividend is worth more than the time value given up, exercising early pays.

Intrinsic value = stock price - strike          (0 if out of the money)
Time value      = call price - intrinsic value
Risk is high when: dividend > time value

The more exact test: the put at the same strike

The call's time value is the common stand-in, and it errs towards warning. The precise comparison is with the put at the same strike and expiration: by exercising, the holder gives up the protection the call gave them, and the put is what that protection costs. When the dividend is larger than that put, early exercise pays. Enter the put's price and the calculator uses it instead.

Worked example

Hypothetical: a stock at $52 pays a $0.72 dividend tomorrow. You are short the $45 call, which trades at $7.30.

  • Intrinsic value: $52 - $45 = $7.00.
  • Time value: $7.30 - $7.00 = $0.30.
  • Test: the $0.72 dividend is larger than the $0.30 of time value, so a holder is better off exercising today and collecting the dividend. Early assignment is likely.
  • At stake: $72.00 per contract: missed on a covered call, or owed on the short call of a spread.

What it means for your position

Covered call
Your shares are sold at the strike and you keep the premium, but you do not receive the dividend. The result is the same as being called at expiration, a few days early.
Call spread
You are left short 100 shares per contract on the ex-date and owe the dividend on them. Your long call still caps the price risk, but not the dividend, so the loss can exceed the spread's max loss by that amount.

Traders who want to avoid it usually close or roll the call before the ex-date. The option roll calculator shows what a roll does to the numbers.

What the calculator does not tell you

  • Exactly who is assigned. Exercise notices are allocated among short holders; a likely exercise does not mean every short contract is assigned.
  • Interest and borrow costs. High interest rates make early call exercise slightly less attractive, and hard-to-borrow stocks can be exercised for other reasons.
  • Assignment at other times. Deep in-the-money options with almost no time value can be assigned on any day, dividend or not.

Frequently asked questions

When is a short call likely to be assigned early?

When it is in the money just before an ex-dividend date and the dividend is larger than the call's remaining time value. Deep in-the-money calls close to expiration, with almost no time value left, are the usual candidates.

How do you calculate the time value of an option?

Subtract the intrinsic value from the option's price. For a call, intrinsic value is the stock price minus the strike, or zero if the call is out of the money. What is left is time value, also called extrinsic value.

Why compare the dividend with the put at the same strike?

Because that put prices what the call holder gives up by exercising: the protection against the stock falling below the strike. When the dividend is worth more than that put, exercising early pays the holder.

What happens if my covered call is assigned early?

Your shares are sold at the strike the next business day and you keep the premium you were paid. You do not receive the upcoming dividend, because you no longer own the shares on the ex-date.

What happens if the short call in my spread is assigned early?

You are short 100 shares per contract and owe the dividend on them. Your long call still limits the price risk; closing the stock and the long call, or exercising the long call, resolves the position.

Can puts be assigned early too?

Yes, mainly deep in-the-money puts with little time value, more often when interest rates are high. Dividends work the other way for puts: a put holder usually waits until after the ex-date, when the stock drops by the dividend.
