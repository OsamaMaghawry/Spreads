How a covered call works

A covered call follows the same few steps every time.

  1. Own the shares. 100 shares for each contract you plan to sell, already held or bought at the same time as the call.
  2. Sell the call. Pick a strike, usually above the current price, and an expiration. The premium is paid into your account at once and is yours to keep whatever happens next.
  3. Below the strike at expiration. The call expires worthless. You keep the shares and the premium, and can sell another call.
  4. Above the strike at expiration. The shares are called away: sold at the strike. You keep the premium and the gain up to the strike, but not any rise above it.
  5. Before expiration. You can buy the call back at any time to keep the shares, or roll it to a later date or a higher strike.

How to calculate covered call returns

A covered call is 100 shares you own and one call you sell against them, per contract. There are two outcomes worth measuring at expiration: the stock finishes above the strike and the shares are called away, or it finishes where it is and you keep both the shares and the premium.

Return if called = (strike - stock price + premium) / stock price
Return if flat   = premium / stock price        (strike at or above the price)
Annualized       = return × 365 / days to expiration
Breakeven        = stock price - premium
Downside cushion = premium / stock price
Max profit       = (strike - stock price + premium) × 100 × contracts

If the strike is below the stock price, a flat stock still means the shares are called away, so the two returns are the same. The calculator handles that case.

Worked example

Hypothetical: you buy 100 shares at $50 and sell the $52.50 call expiring in 30 days for $1.10.

  • Premium: $1.10 × 100 = $110.00, received when the call is sold.
  • If called: ($52.50 - $50 + $1.10) / $50 = 7.2% in 30 days, 87.6% annualized. That is $360.00, the most this position can make.
  • If flat: $1.10 / $50 = 2.2%, 26.8% annualized, and you still own the shares.
  • Breakeven: $50 - $1.10 = $48.90. Below that, the shares lose more than the premium brought in.

Which stock price to enter

Enter what you are paying for the shares when you buy them and sell the call together. If you already own the shares, today's price measures the call from here, which is how most traders compare one call with another; the price you originally paid measures the whole position since you bought it. Some calculators divide by the stock price minus the premium instead, which gives slightly higher figures for the same trade.

What the calculator does not tell you

  • Annualizing is a comparison, not a forecast. It assumes you could sell the same call on the same terms again and again with no losing months. Use it to compare a 7 day call with a 45 day call, not to estimate a year.
  • The downside is the stock's. The premium cushions a fall but does not cap it: below the breakeven, every dollar the stock drops is a dollar lost per share.
  • Dividends and early assignment. Dividends are not included. A call in the money before an ex-dividend date can be assigned early; the early assignment calculator checks for that.

Frequently asked questions

What is a covered call?

Selling a call option on shares you already own, one contract per 100 shares. You are paid a premium up front, and in return agree to sell the shares at the strike if the stock is above it at expiration. The shares cover the call, which is where the name comes from.

How does a covered call work?

You own 100 shares and sell one call against them, collecting the premium up front. If the stock finishes below the strike, the call expires and you keep the shares and the premium. If it finishes above, your shares are sold at the strike and you keep the premium plus the gain up to the strike.

Why do traders sell covered calls?

To collect premium on shares they hold, in exchange for giving up any gain above the strike. The premium lowers the breakeven a little, but it does not protect against a large fall in the stock.

How do you calculate a covered call return?

For the shares being called away: strike minus stock price plus premium, divided by the stock price. For the stock staying flat: premium divided by the stock price. Both assume you hold to expiration.

How do you annualize a covered call return?

Multiply the return by 365 and divide by the days to expiration. A return of 2.2% over 30 days annualizes to 26.8%. Read it as a way to compare calls of different lengths, not as a yearly figure.

What is the breakeven on a covered call?

The stock price minus the premium received. If you buy at $50 and sell a call for $1.10, you lose money at expiration only if the stock finishes below $48.90.

What is the max profit on a covered call?

The strike minus the stock price plus the premium, times 100 per contract. Above the strike the shares are called away, so any further rise in the stock is not yours.

What happens if my covered call is assigned early?

Your shares are sold at the strike and you keep the premium. Early assignment usually happens just before an ex-dividend date, when the call's remaining time value is smaller than the dividend.

Should I use the price I paid or today's price?

Today's price measures the new call on its own, which is the usual way to compare calls. The price you paid measures the position since you bought the shares, including any gain or loss already on them.
