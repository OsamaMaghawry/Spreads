How to calculate a credit spread

A credit spread is two options on the same stock and expiration: you sell one and buy one further from the money, and you are paid a credit for the pair. Every figure above comes from the two strikes and that credit.

Width          = short strike - long strike   (as a positive number)
Max profit     = credit × 100 × contracts
Max loss       = (width - credit) × 100 × contracts
Breakeven, put  = short strike - credit
Breakeven, call = short strike + credit
Return on risk = max profit / max loss
Max profit
The credit you received, times 100 shares per contract. You keep all of it if both options expire worthless.
Max loss
Width minus credit, times 100. On a $5 spread sold for $1.20 that is $3.80 a share, or $380 a contract.
Breakeven
For a put credit spread, the short strike minus the credit. For a call credit spread, the short strike plus the credit.
Return on risk
Max profit divided by max loss. It says what the trade pays for what it puts at stake, not how likely it is to pay.
Collateral
What a broker typically holds while the spread is open: the width times 100. Some brokers reduce that by the credit received, which makes it equal to the max loss.

Worked example: a put credit spread

Hypothetical: a stock trades at $100. You sell the 95 put and buy the 90 put for a $1.20 credit, one contract. You collect $120.00. If the stock finishes above $95, both puts expire worthless and you keep it. If it finishes below $90, the spread is worth its full $5 and you lose $380.00. Between the strikes the result moves dollar for dollar, crossing zero at $93.80.

Worked example: a call credit spread

Hypothetical: the same $100 stock. You sell the 105 call and buy the 110 call for $1.10. Max profit is $110.00, max loss is $390.00, and the breakeven is $106.10, the short strike plus the credit. Return on risk is 28.2%.

What the calculator does not tell you

  • The odds. Return on risk is not a probability. A high return on risk usually means the short strike is close to the stock price.
  • The path before expiration. These are expiration figures. Before then the spread is marked at its market price, which can show a loss bigger than you expect while the stock is between the strikes.
  • Fees and assignment. Commissions and fees are not included. A short option can be assigned early, most often around a dividend, which turns one leg into shares until you act.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate the max loss on a credit spread?

Subtract the credit from the width between the strikes, then multiply by 100 and by the number of contracts. A $5 wide spread sold for $1.20 has a max loss of $3.80 a share, $380 per contract.

How do you calculate the breakeven of a put credit spread?

Short put strike minus the credit received. For a call credit spread it is the short call strike plus the credit. At expiration the position makes money on the safe side of that price and loses on the other.

Is a bull put spread the same as a put credit spread?

Yes. A bull put spread and a put credit spread are the same position: sell a put, buy a lower strike put. A bear call spread is the same as a call credit spread: sell a call, buy a higher strike call.

How much collateral does a credit spread need?

Usually the width of the spread times 100 per contract. Some brokers reduce it by the credit you received, so the amount held equals the max loss. Your broker's figure is the one that applies to your account.

Can a credit spread lose more than its max loss?

Not at expiration, before fees. Before expiration the screen shows a mark, and an early assignment on the short leg can briefly turn it into a stock position, which is covered in what max loss covers before expiration.

Does return on risk tell me the chance of profit?

No. Return on risk compares what the spread pays with what it can lose. A spread with a short strike close to the stock price pays more for the same risk precisely because it is more likely to finish against you.
